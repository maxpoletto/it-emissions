\section*{Abstract}
Information technology (IT), including rapidly expanding artificial intelligence
(AI) services, is a necessary component of university net-zero planning. We
present a parameterizable model of IT emissions on a university campus,
developed in the context of the University of Zürich's net-zero initiative. The
model approximates operational and lifecycle emissions of personal devices,
university-owned computing and network infrastructure, cloud computing,
supercomputing, cloud productivity services, and generative-AI workloads. Our
analysis shows that embodied emissions of end-user devices are a major component
of overall IT emissions, while operational impacts are surprisingly modest.
Emissions from simple chatbot use remain small in most scenarios, but agentic AI
workflows such as coding assistants can lead to substantial emissions if adopted
heavily. These results support a cleaner-IT strategy centered on device lifetime
extension, transparent cloud procurement, and governance of high-intensity AI
workloads.

\section{Introduction}

Information technology accounts for over 5\% of global electricity consumption
and at least 1.7\% of global carbon emissions~\cite{wb:itu:ict}. At the
University of Zürich (UZH), which has over 38,000 students and staff and is the
largest Swiss university, a recent sustainability report estimated that IT
accounted for about 1.5~\ktcoe\ of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in 2024,
approximately 6.3\% of total university
emissions~\cite{uzh:sustainability:report}. The report takes into account
university-owned workstations, servers, and audiovisual equipment, but does not
include other IT-related items such as personal devices, networking, cloud
computing, or the growing use of artificial intelligence.

AI applications are being adopted at historically high rates, more quickly than
the personal computer or the internet were adopted in the
past~\cite{bick:ai:adoption}. Enterprise AI adoption has accelerated, with
generative AI tools seeing widespread deployment across industries within months
of their release~\cite{mckinsey2024ai}. In July 2025, ChatGPT received 2.5
billion queries per day, up from 1 billion in December 2024, and within an order
of magnitude of Google's approximately 15 billion queries per
day~\cite{techcrunch:chatgpt}. AI growth is currently projected to increase the
share of global electricity consumption for data centers from $\sim 1\%$ in 2024
to $\sim 3\%$ by 2030~\cite{iea:ai:energy,iea:keyquestions:ai}.

The character of generative-AI use is also changing. Early consumer use was
dominated by short chatbot interactions, for which  estimates converge around
fractions of a watt-hour to a few watt-hours per request. Newer agentic systems,
including research agents and coding assistants such as Codex and Claude Code,
can consume on the order of kilowatt-hours per user-day because they issue many
sequential long-context
requests~\cite{jegham2025hungry,ritchie:ai:electricity:2026,couch:claude:code:energy}.
A campus-level assessment therefore needs to distinguish ordinary LLM chat from
agentic AI workflows.

The University of Zürich has set itself the target of becoming climate-neutral
by 2030 within the categories of energy, transportation, food, and waste, with
additional goals in 2035 and beyond~\cite{uzh:sustainability:report}. Given the
current sizable contribution of IT to the university's emissions, and the
potential for growth through AI adoption in the coming years, we felt it was
necessary to understand IT emissions and their growth trajectories in more
detail.

This paper presents a simple interactive model of IT emissions on a university
campus. We developed it in the specific context of the University of Zürich, but
believe that it is parameterizable and general enough to apply to other similar
university campuses or large corporate facilities.

We seek to answer three broad questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item What are the main contributors to the IT emissions of a university?
  \item Could migrating some on-campus IT services to commercial cloud providers
  decrease the overall campus footprint?
  \item What is the likely impact of the surge in AI adoption, and under what
  usage levels do chatbots or agentic AI become a material source of IT
  emissions?
\end{itemize}

Our model sets relatively broad system boundaries. It takes into account
university-owned equipment but also university services hosted by commercial
cloud providers and university use of commercial generative AI products. It also
accounts for students' personal devices, since they are key to student work and
constitute the bulk of the university's end-user devices. We attempt to model
devices' full lifecycle impact, including manufacture and disposal. 

Experts rightly point to the many potential benefits of AI in the areas of
climate change mitigation and adaptation~\cite{climate:change:ai}. On a
university campus, these might include more efficient building heating and
cooling, improved transportation logistics, and reduced administrative overhead.
However, those benefits are uncertain, indirect, and likely to interact with
rebound effects. In this paper, we consider only the direct impacts of AI
in terms of electricity consumption and associated hardware emissions.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:model:methodology} presents the general modeling framework,
including system boundaries and assumptions. Section~\ref{sec:model:details}
justifies some of the specific parameter values used in the model.
Section~\ref{sec:results} describes results and explores different scenarios and
assumptions. Section~\ref{sec:recommendations} offers recommendations to
university IT administrators. Section~\ref{sec:related:work} describes related
work in this area.

\section{Modeling methodology}
\label{sec:model:methodology}

Our aim is to model energy use from university IT and AI broadly, without
restricting ourselves to university-owned devices, specific IT departments, etc.
At the same time, we want to be able to distinguish the contributions of
different factors, such as student-owned devices or LLMs, and to compare the
model's output against existing university data points (energy bills, etc.).

\subsection{System boundaries}

We define the boundaries of our model as follows.

\subsubsection*{Population}

We include IT-related activity from all members of the UZH community: faculty,
staff, and students.

\subsubsection*{On-campus devices}

Student-owned devices, such as laptops and smartphones, are in scope, since they
are the primary means by which students access university IT services and do
their coursework. Given the duration of studies, we include personal devices'
embodied emissions in the model, and do not try to apportion them further
between university and private use.

University-owned devices are in scope. We have accurate numbers for staff
workstations, classroom computers, network access points, and audiovisual
devices managed by the central IT department. We can only estimate other
devices, such as networking equipment or computers managed by individual
research groups. We include these devices' embodied emissions in the model.

For servers, which are mostly located in on-premise data centers, we also
attempt to take into account emissions from cooling. (We do not model the
manufacturing emissions of the cooling equipment itself.)

\subsubsection*{Cloud and supercomputing facilities}

UZH at present does not make significant use of commercial cloud compute, but
the model allows simulating the shift of some fraction of the server load to
cloud VMs. For this hypothetical cloud footprint, like for on-premise data
centers, we try to model operational emissions, embodied emissions, and
emissions from cooling.

Application-level cloud services such as Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace are
ubiquitous but difficult to model bottom-up without tenant-level vendor reports.
We use a rough composite emissions factor per user, with provider overhead
implicit in that factor; hardware emissions cannot be separated from it.

UZH faculties use shared off-campus supercomputing facilities, primarily the
Swiss National Supercomputing Center, CSCS~\cite{cscs}. Both operational impacts
and embodied emissions are in scope for our analysis.

\subsubsection*{Generative and agentic AI}

We distinguish ordinary chatbot use, parameterized by queries per active
user-day and energy per query, from agentic workflows, parameterized by energy
per active user-day. We attempt to account separately for inference, training,
and embodied emissions, applying facility overhead where it is not already
included in the energy estimate.

\subsection{Accounting approach}

\subsubsection*{On-campus devices}

We model campus device emissions bottom-up. We first identify categories of
devices (phones, laptops, workstations, monitors, routers, etc.). For each
device type, we research a range of common models, then use manufacturer data
sheets or other available documentation to identify operational power draw (in
watts) as well as embodied emissions related to manufacture and disposal (in
\kgcoe) per device. Based on this data, we model the power draw and embodied
emissions of an ``exemplar'' device of each type.

For each device type we also estimate (1) a count (\eg, a laptop for every
student and staff), (2) an estimated duty cycle (\eg, laptops used 8 hours per
day, 200 days per year), and (3) a useful lifetime (in years).

Given all this, for each device type we can straightforwardly obtain an annual
energy use (kWh) and annual embodied emissions (embodied emissions amortized
over useful lifetime) (\kgcoe). We then estimate the local grid's carbon
emissions intensity (\gcoekwh) to convert the energy use to emissions and obtain
a combined total annual emissions number. We use deterministic mean device
footprints; the variability of individual products does not by itself define
uncertainty in the population mean.

The Swiss Federal Office for the Environment has an extensive report on the
environmental impact of electricity generation in
Switzerland~\cite{krebs2018umweltbilanz}, but we are fortunate to also have
hourly emissions intensity data from \textcite{electricitymaps}. We use the
hourly intensity data for {\em consumption} (not production) to compute
emissions intensity for every hour of the day averaged over all days in 2024.
Emissions vary from a high of 64 \gcoekwh\ in the middle of the night to a low
of 45 \gcoekwh\ in the middle of the day. This variation is sufficiently large
that we refine the model's device duty cycle to represent four six-hour periods
over the course of the day. It allows us to specify, for example, that user
devices are mostly used during the day whereas servers run around the clock.

For servers (in on-premise data centers) we also account for cooling overhead
using historical electricity use data.

\subsubsection*{Cloud and supercomputing facilities}

We model a hypothetical shift of some workloads from servers in an on-premise
data center to a commercial cloud. We model the shift as: the fraction of
servers that are moved to the cloud; the average conversion ratio of on-premise
servers to cloud servers; cloud data center power usage effectiveness (PUE), the
ratio of total facility electricity use to IT electricity use; and electricity
emissions intensity, since the cloud data center may be located in a different
region than the on-premise data center.

Vendors of application-level cloud
services~\cite{microsoft:m365:emissions,google:workspace:carbon} provide
per-tenant carbon accounting tools, but UZH has no such data. As a placeholder,
we model a wide range of possible emissions per user, from 1 to 10 \kgcoe/year.

For supercomputing facilities, primarily CSCS, we estimate both the operational
impact of workloads and a range of likely embodied emissions based on the UZH
allocation of total CSCS electricity use and an approximate model of server
hardware. Since we lack a detailed hardware inventory for CSCS, we cannot model
embodied emissions precisely bottom-up.

For each compute environment, PUE multiplies IT electricity once; inputs that
already cover the whole facility receive no further multiplier. PUE does not
multiply hardware emissions. The local, cloud, supercomputing, and AI electricity
factors are independently configurable. The SaaS factor is a composite estimate,
so no separate PUE or electricity factor is applied to it.

\subsubsection*{Generative and agentic AI}

For LLMs, we estimate operational energy use using current estimates in the
literature of state-of-the-art
models~\cite{devries2023growing,epoch2025howmuchenergydoeschatgptuse,jegham2025hungry}.
Simple text-query scenarios are represented in Wh per query. Agentic scenarios
are represented in kWh per active user-day, because coding and research agents
combine many model calls, tool calls, context-cache reads and writes, and long
outputs into one work
session~\cite{ritchie:ai:electricity:2026,couch:claude:code:energy}.

We represent training as a separate allocation on top of operational inference
emissions. Embodied hardware emissions are allocated per query for ordinary
chatbot use and per kWh of estimated facility inference electricity for
agentic workflows. Because AI providers do not publish workload-level energy data, the
AI scenarios are presented as sensitivity ranges.

These AI estimates cover externally hosted services; workloads already
represented in campus, cloud-compute, or CSCS totals are not added again.

\subsection{Treatment of uncertainty}

Many parameters are defined by a central estimate and upper and lower bounds of
a plausible range. We evaluate combinations of these bounds to report ranges of
possible outcomes. Figures display these ranges as whiskers: these are
deterministic assumption ranges, not confidence intervals.

\section{Model details}
\label{sec:model:details}

The previous section describes our overall modeling approach, which we believe
is broadly applicable to university and other large campus settings. This
section presents the reasoning behind specific values we employ in the UZH
evaluation.

\subsection{On-campus devices}

\subsubsection*{Number of devices}

We have an authoritative census of devices (workstations, servers, audiovisual
equipment) managed by central IT, as follows.

  \end{tabular}
  \label{tab:oncampusdevices}
\end{center}

% point 3
In addition to these numbers, which cover staff and facilities, we assume that
80\% of the student population of 28,000 people have a laptop (or equivalent
device, such as a tablet), and that everyone at the university has a mobile
phone, resulting in a rough estimate of 32,000 laptops and 35,000 mobile phones.

Traditional desktop computers appear to be infrequent on campus. By default we
set their number to zero in our model, but we still provide the framework to
model them if desired.

The device lifetime for university-managed laptops and monitors comes from UZH's
central IT department and is remarkably long. For other types of devices we
assume a similarly conservative management of hardware resources. We also
estimate that student-owned laptops have a shorter lifetime of 5 years, and
therefore, for the purposes of the results later in this paper, we set the
default average lifetime of laptops in our model to 6 years. These long
lifetimes make embodied emissions less dominant than they would be under shorter
refresh cycles, since manufacturing emissions are amortized over more years.

\subsubsection*{Operational emissions}

The Swiss electricity mix is substantially cleaner than the European or North
American average, so generic manufacturer estimates of lifetime usage-related
emissions are unsuitable. Unfortunately, in manufacturer documentation we found
surprisingly few sources of data on device power consumption, as opposed to
estimated lifetime CO$_2$ emissions. Based on some manufacturer data sheets,
primarily from Apple, Cisco, and Dell, we model power draw of different devices
while under load using the following initial parameters:

%point 7
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{|l|r|r|}
    \hline
    \textbf{Device} & \textbf{Mean (W)} & \textbf{$\sigma$ (W)} \\ \hline
    Laptop & 50 & 20 \\ \hline
    Monitor & 50 & 10 \\ \hline
    Desktop & 100 & 20 \\ \hline
    Server & 400 & 100 \\ \hline
    Mobile phone & 10 & 2 \\ \hline
    A/V equipment & 250 & 50 \\ \hline
    Printer & 1000 & 200 \\ \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

Our assumptions about device duty cycle can be summarized as follows: (1)
end-user devices are used during the day on weekdays (250 days per year), (2)
servers run around-the-clock but at 80\% of peak power load, (3) mobile phones
are modeled through their charging energy rather than as continuously plugged-in
devices, and (4) printers are idle except for a few minutes per day. A typical
smartphone battery capacity of 15--20 Wh, charged once per day with 80--90\%
charger efficiency, corresponds to roughly 20--25 Wh/day. In the model this is
represented conservatively as a 10 W charging load for a three-hour period
(30 Wh/day) on 250 days/year. Separate standby power is not included in the
calculation. This omission is most relevant for printers, which can draw
50--100 W while idle.

For campus networking, UZH has approximately 4,000 wifi access points. A
bottom-up access-point estimate is 4,000 devices $\times$ 15 W $\times$ 8,760
h/year $\approx$ 526 MWh/year, corresponding to roughly 25--35 \tcoe/year under
the Swiss electricity mix. Core switches, distribution switches, routers,
controllers, and storage networking are less well inventoried. We approximate
the upstream network as 10\% of the access-point count, or 400
switch/router-class devices. At 100--250 W each, this adds approximately
350--876 MWh/year. Including additional uncertainty from controllers, PoE
losses, and storage networking, we retain an operational range of roughly
50--100 \tcoe/year. The default inventory uses 250 W per switch/router and
produces \ModelNetworkOperationalT{} \tcoe/year. The plotted operational range
varies that electricity estimate by a factor of 0.67--1.33.

Based on limited UZH historical power data, we estimate that the power usage
effectiveness of on-premise data centers is approximately 2. This value, higher
than the commercial average of 1.56~\cite{google:datacenter:efficiency}, is
likely attributable to inefficient cooling.

For several device types, especially end-user devices such as phones and
laptops, embodied emissions comprise a majority of lifetime emissions. Different
sources report widely different values for these emissions. Our approach has
been to find several studies and industry references, and then estimate a mean
reference value as well as likely minimum and maximum values.

For laptops, the numbers from Ecoinvent, a reputable proprietary Swiss life
cycle inventory database used in the UZH sustainability report, appear to be on
the low end. The most comprehensive publicly available data comes from
\textcite{rarecoil}, which lists manufacturer information for over 90 popular
devices. Mean supply chain emissions in this data set are 244 \kgcoe\ with a
standard deviation of 128 kg. To be conservative, we split the difference
between this number and the Ecoinvent values and model laptops embodied
emissions at 180 \kgcoe\ / device.

For desktops, most estimates are in the range of 250 - 400 \kgcoe. The most
detailed data comes from Dell's Product Carbon Footprints~\cite{dellpcf}, which
the company publishes for each of their products. A sample of 15 desktops has
mean supply chain emissions of 289 \kgcoe\ with $\sigma = 80$ kg. We model
desktop embodied emissions at 290 \kgcoe\ / device.

In the case of servers, \textcite{davy2021} reports manufacturer data for many
Dell servers and estimates for different types of EC2 instances. The former have
mean supply chain emissions of 1252 \kgcoe\ with $\sigma = 330$ kg, the latter
1912 kg with $\sigma = 885$ kg. \textcite{boavizta:api} reports mean supply
chain emissions of 899 \kgcoe\ for a medium-sized server. We model server supply
chain emissions at 1100 \kgcoe\ / device.

The variability of estimates for mobile phones is much smaller. We model phone
embodied emissions at 50 \kgcoe\ / device.

We found less data for other types of devices. We model them as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Computer monitors: 340 \kgcoe~\cite{dellpcf,lovehagen2023,teehan2013}.
  \item Conference room displays: 700 \kgcoe\ (scaling monitor by
  $(40''/27'')^2$). We use this as a proxy for all audiovisual equipment
  (lecture room projectors, etc.). Note that the numbers are sufficiently low
  that errors in this estimate do not significantly affect the overall result.
  \item Printer/copier stations: 1100 \kgcoe~\cite{ecoinvent}.
  \item Network equipment: \textcite{lovehagen2023} estimate 30 \kgcoe\ for
  customer-premises equipment, a category that is close to wireless access
  points. We use 50 \kgcoe\ per access point as a conservative value. For
  upstream switches and routers, we again use 10\% of the access-point count and
  approximate each device with the embodied emissions of a desktop PC, 290
  \kgcoe. Over an 8-year lifetime, this gives annual embodied emissions of
  $(4{,}000 \times 50 + 400 \times 290) / 8 = 39{,}500$ \kgcoe/year.
  Studies of other network equipment attribute 80--95\% of lifecycle emissions
  to usage~\cite{cisco2024,jacob2023}, but those shares depend on electricity mix
  and utilization. Hardware is about a third of our Swiss-grid network estimate;
  its uncertainty has not been quantified.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cloud and supercomputing facilities}

The secretiveness of major cloud providers makes it difficult to estimate the
embodied and operational emissions of cloud infrastructure. The best resource we
have found is the Datavizta API by the Boavizta working
group~\cite{boavizta:api}.

%point 4
Lacking information about UZH's workloads, we model a hypothetical UZH cloud
footprint as the replacement of one campus server with a configurable number (by
default, one) of Azure {\tt standard\_d48s\_v3} instances (Intel Xeon, 48 cores,
192 GB RAM, 2TB SSD)~\cite{msftvms}. Based on data from Boavizta, such a machine
has embodied emissions of 667 \kgcoe\ and mean electricity power usage of 255 W.
Given the uncertainty, we round up to 700 \kgcoe\ and 300 W. We assume
deployment to Microsoft Azure's
``\href{https://datacenters.microsoft.com/globe/explore?info=region_switzerlandnorth}{Switzerland
North}'' region, which makes sense for a Swiss university and means that the
electricity mix is the same as for on-premise facilities. We estimate a power
usage effectiveness of 1.15 for this facility. (As of 2025, average PUE for
cloud computing is 1.56, with Google averaging 1.09 across its
fleet~\cite{google:datacenter:efficiency} and Microsoft ranging from 1.14 to
1.19 in Europe~\cite{microsoft:datacenter:efficiency}.)

Given all these parameters, we can then compute cloud server operational
emissions, embodied emissions, and cooling overhead as we do for on-premise
servers.

As mentioned above, for application-level cloud services such as Microsoft 365
and Google Workspace, we use a range of 1--10 \kgcoe/user/year. For a population
of 38,000 users, this corresponds to 38--380 \tcoe/year. The figures use the
midpoint of this range, 209 \tcoe/year. This estimate is intentionally separated
from the higher-confidence campus device and server results.

For supercomputing at CSCS, recent information~\cite{cscs:supercomputing}
indicates total compute electricity use of approximately 60 GWh/year and an
estimated UZH allocation of approximately 3.6 GWh/year, or about 6\% of the
total. Using a separate Swiss electricity factor of 50 \gcoekwh, this corresponds to
approximately 180 \tcoe/year of operational emissions. Since the 3.6 GWh/year
figure is treated as compute-only under our working assumption, a PUE range
of 1.1--1.3 adds 18--54 \tcoe/year; PUE 1.2 gives 216 \tcoe/year including
facility overhead. For embodied
emissions, since we lack a precise hardware inventory, we use a rough
server-equivalent estimate. The UZH electricity allocation corresponds to an
average allocated power of $3.6$ GWh / 8,760 h $\approx 411$ kW. Assuming that
this load is represented by server nodes drawing 0.4--2.0 kW each, and that, per
the analysis above, each node has embodied emissions of 1.1 \tcoe\ and a 5-year
lifetime, amortized embodied emissions fall in the range of 45--226 \tcoe/year.
At a central server-equivalent power of 1.2 kW, the model calculates
\ModelCscsHardwareT{} \tcoe/year of embodied emissions. This is not the midpoint
of the output range, because the hardware estimate is inversely proportional
to node power. The plotted CSCS total is \ModelCscsTotalT{} \tcoe/year, with a
\ModelCscsLowT{}--\ModelCscsHighT{} \tcoe/year assumption range from PUE and
node power. Allocation, accelerator mix, and lifetime uncertainty remain outside
these bounds.

\subsection{Generative and agentic AI}

We model ordinary chatbot use and agentic workflows separately because their
natural units differ. Chatbot use is reasonably approximated in Wh/query, while
agentic coding and research sessions are better approximated in kWh per active
user-day.

\subsubsection*{Chatbot use}

A widely cited 2023 study~\cite{devries2023growing} estimated the average energy
consumption of a large language model like ChatGPT at approximately 3
Wh/request, about ten times the energy of a standard Google search query. In
2025, \textcite{epoch2025howmuchenergydoeschatgptuse} argued that the energy use
for the average GPT-4o query was closer to 0.3 Wh.
\textcite{ritchie:ai:electricity:2026}, summarizing IEA and benchmark data,
places simple text queries around 0.1--0.6 Wh. However,
\textcite{jegham2025hungry} finds strong workload dependence: their benchmarked
long prompts exceed 29 Wh for the most energy-intensive models and vary by more
than a factor of 60 across systems. We assume 1 Wh/query as our ordinary-chat
default and vary this from 0.3--3 Wh/query in sensitivity scenarios. For the
purposes of this report, we treat query energy estimates as facility-wide and do
not apply PUE again.

Due to the increasing size and complexity of state-of-the-art models, the power
required to train such models has been doubling
annually~\cite{epoch2024powerusagetrend}. The literature offers little insight
into the relative electricity consumption of training and
inference~\cite{verdecchia2023systematic}.
Google~\cite{patterson:footprint:2022} reports that in 2019--2021 inference
accounted for about 60\% of AI energy consumption, implying a ratio of training
energy to inference energy of 2:3, though that ratio includes Google's internal
reserch and development. Meta's model card for Llama
3.1~\cite{meta:llama31:modelcard} reports 30.84 million GPU-hours for the 405B
model on GPUs rated at 700 W. Multiplying GPU-hours by this nameplate power
gives approximately 21.6 GWh. We assume a combined factor of 1.4 for host-server
electricity and data-center overhead, giving a rough facility-level training
estimate of 30 GWh. Assuming ChatGPT's reported 2.5 billion queries per
day~\cite{techcrunch:chatgpt} and 0.3 Wh/query, inference energy is
approximately 750 MWh/day, or 203 GWh over an assumed nine-month model
deployment lifetime. This gives a training-to-inference ratio of $30:203 \approx
1:7$. At our default of 1 Wh/query, inference energy would instead be
approximately 675 GWh over the same period, and the ratio would be about $1:22$.
Given these order-of-magnitude illustrations, and wanting to be conservative in
not underestimating training overhead, we set our training overhead allocation
to 20\% of inference costs.

For chatbot embodied emissions, we use a bottom-up GPU allocation.
\textcite{epoch2025howmuchenergydoeschatgptuse} estimates 1 second of NVIDIA
H100 GPU time per query. NVIDIA reports~\cite{nvidia:h100} a cradle-to-gate
carbon footprint of 1,312 \kgcoe\ for an HGX H100 baseboard containing eight
GPUs. We use a working whole-server footprint of 2,000 \kgcoe, including that
baseboard, and divide by eight to allocate 250 \kgcoe\ per GPU, including its
share of the host server~\cite{nvidia:dgx}. If this platform is used for three
years at 70\% utilization~\cite{tomshardware:2024}, we obtain direct embodied
emissions of approximately 0.004 \gcoe/query~\footnote{70\% utilization for
three years is approximately 18,000 hours; at one GPU-second per query, that
equates to 65M queries.}. The model uses 0.1 \gcoe/query as a very conservative
default to allow for lower utilization and allocation uncertainty.

The facilities hosting LLMs are distributed and the location that serves a
particular request is unknown. To map energy use to emissions we use the average
emissions intensity of electricity generation in the EU in 2023, 207
\gcoekwh~\cite{eea:emissions:2025}. This is higher than the Swiss campus
electricity intensity, but an illustrative proxy for unknown serving locations.

In summary, for chatbot use, we make the following default and sensitivity assumptions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Ordinary chat: default 15 queries/user/day at 1 Wh/query; sensitivity
  range 0.3--3 Wh/query and 10--30 queries/user/day.
  \item Training overhead of 20\% on operational inference energy.
  \item Carbon emission intensity of 207 \gcoekwh.
  \item Embodied emissions of 0.1 \gcoe/query.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Agentic AI use}

Agentic or reasoning workflows have far different energy requirements.
\textcite{ritchie:ai:electricity:2026} describes a heavy-use example of 24
agentic tasks/day at 100 Wh/task, hence 2.4 kWh/day.
\textcite{couch:claude:code:energy} presents a non-peer-reviewed but nonetheless
useful methodology that derives token volumes from local Claude Code logs and
infers a median coding-agent session of 41 Wh and a heavy coding-agent workday
of approximately 1.3 kWh. We use 1.5 kWh/day as a central estimate, with a range
of 1--3 kWh/day. As with the chatbot case, we treat these energy estimates as
facility inference electricity, including data-center overhead. We add training
as a separate allocation.

For amortized hardware emissions, the 250 \kgcoe per-GPU allocation given above,
spread over roughly 18,000 active hours and with allocated facility power of 1
kW, implies 14 \gcoekwh. Again, being deliberately cautious, we assume 30
\gcoekwh, with a range of 10--50 \gcoekwh in sensitivity scenarios.

Summarizing, in the agentic case we have:

\begin{itemize}
  \item 1.5 kWh/day per active user, with a range of 1--3 kWh/day.
  \item Training overhead of 20\% on operational inference energy.
  \item 30 \gcoekwh\ amortized hardware emissions, with a range of 10--50
  \gcoekwh.
  \item Carbon emission intensity of 207 \gcoekwh.
\end{itemize}

Agent-generated requests are excluded from the ordinary-chat allowance.
A central, illustrative estimate of emissions per active agentic user per day
is therefore as follows. The factor $(1+0.20)$ accounts for the training
allocation; facility overhead is already included in the daily energy input:

\[ 1.5~\mathrm{kWh/day} \times \bigl[207 \times (1+0.20) + 30\bigr]~\mathrm{gCO_2e/kWh} \approx 0.4~\mathrm{kgCO_2e/day}. \]

Then, for example, assuming that 10\% of the UZH population uses agentic AI
heavily on 250 workdays per year, we obtain

\[ 0.1 \times 38{,}000~\mathrm{users} \times 0.4~\mathrm{kgCO_2e/(user-day)} \times 250~\mathrm{days/year} \approx 380~\mathrm{tCO_2e/year}. \]

For sensitivity analysis, we vary adoption from 5\% to 20\% of the population
(1,900--7,600 users). These adoption bounds, together with the daily energy and
hardware-factor bounds, enter the annual-bar whiskers.

\subsection{Model limitations}
% point 6
Our biggest limitation is the relative lack of ground truth data about UZH IT
systems. We have obtained only estimates of campus data center energy use, and
limited information about UZH research workloads, SaaS use, and external
high-performance computing infrastructure.

Additionally, our work relies on assumptions and anecdotal evidence about device
ownership and use among the university population. The largest remaining
behavioral uncertainty is AI usage, especially the adoption rate of agentic
workflows. We therefore avoid a single point prediction for agentic AI and
instead report adoption and intensity thresholds.

Moreover, cloud vendors and AI companies are secretive about their
infrastructure, and we have had to rely on public datasets, vendor tools, and
research literature to estimate cloud and AI emissions. Microsoft and Google
tenant-level emissions reports, and provider-level energy-per-task disclosures
for AI systems, would substantially reduce uncertainty.

However, all these points of uncertainty are exposed as user-configurable
parameters or explicit scenario ranges in the model, allowing sensitivity
analysis and exploration of alternate contexts.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Sustainability-report comparison}

The UZH 2024 Sustainability Report~\cite{uzh:sustainability:report} provides a
comparator for university-owned devices. Including its separate 81 \tcoe\
audiovisual row gives a total of 1,535 \tcoe. A like-for-like comparison also
requires the report's Ecoinvent~\cite{ecoinvent} footprint factors and device
lifetimes. The exact saved configuration behind our earlier approximately
1,600 \tcoe\ model comparison is unavailable, so we do not treat that result as
reproducible numerical validation. The broader scenarios below have explicit
saved configurations and share their calculation code with the interactive app.

\subsection{Key results}

We now discuss the main takeaways from our model when it is configured with the
parameter values described above. In Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity:analysis} we
explore how these results change under different assumptions.

\subsubsection*{Impact of embodied emissions}

Maybe the most striking result is that, when we consider the entire population
of campus devices, and not only those owned and managed by the university,
end-user devices and embodied emissions are by far the largest source of IT
emissions. See Figure~\ref{fig:baseline}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig-baseline.png}
  \caption{Baseline scenario from the shared calculator. Bars separate embodied hardware, training allocations, and operational emissions (including the composite SaaS factor). Whiskers show assumption ranges for network electricity, SaaS emissions per user, CSCS PUE and server-equivalent power, chatbot query energy and volume, and agent adoption, daily energy and hardware allocations; other uncertainty is not quantified. Central agent use is illustrative: 10\% adoption at 1.5 kWh of facility inference electricity per active user-day.}
  \label{fig:baseline}
\end{figure}

Many studies~\cite{wb:itu:ict,umich:factsheet} agree that a large fraction of
computer and mobile phone energy demand stems from the manufacturing phase. This
is due in part to the fact that such end-user devices are not used continuously.
Our estimate of embodied emissions comprising \ModelLaptopEmbodiedPercent{}\% of total laptop
emissions is near the top of the range in the literature, and it is consistent
with the fact that the carbon intensity of Swiss electricity is about one
quarter the EU average~\cite{electricitymaps}.

\subsubsection*{Impact of cloud computing}

Hypothetically moving all UZH server workloads from on-premise data centers to a
commercial cloud provider eliminates about 170~\tcoe\ of cooling overhead and
replaces about 415~\tcoe\ of on-premise server emissions with about 380~\tcoe\
of cloud emissions. Consequently, total IT emissions fall from approximately
\ModelBaselineKt{}~\ktcoe\ to \ModelCloudKt{}~\ktcoe, a saving of
\ModelCloudSavingT{}~\tcoe/year under these
assumptions. See Figure~\ref{fig:cloud}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig-cloud.png}
  \caption{All local servers migrated one-for-one to cloud instances at PUE 1.15 and 53.75 \gcoekwh. Campus networking and other services remain in the boundary. Whiskers have the same meaning as in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline}; the paired savings calculation holds shared assumptions fixed.}
  \label{fig:cloud}
\end{figure}

For relatively small and inefficiently cooled facilities such as those of UZH,
migration to the cloud certainly improves power usage effectiveness. Whether the
migration is a net win, however, depends on details that would require an
in-depth engagement with the cloud provider. The results above assume a
one-for-one swap of on-premise servers with cloud ones, as well as the same
electricity emissions intensity for the two facilities. If the cloud footprint
needed to be bigger, or if the data center were located in a region with dirtier
electricity, those factors might outweigh the improved cooling efficiency.

There is also a financial argument to consider: if migration to the cloud is
more expensive than continued on-premise operation, the savings from not
migrating might be invested in more cost effective emissions improvements
(including ones not related to IT).

\subsubsection*{Impact of generative and agentic AI}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig-llm-growth.png}
  \caption{Ordinary chatbot growth scenario, excluding agents. Central values assume 15 queries/user/day, initially 1 Wh/query, 250 active days/year, 50\% annual usage growth, 30\% annual reduction in query energy, 50\% annual growth in training emissions, 20\% initial training allocation, and 0.1 \gcoe/query hardware allocation. Whiskers vary initial query energy from 0.3 to 3 Wh and initial query volume from 10 to 30 queries/user/day while holding growth rates and user counts fixed.}
  \label{fig:llm:growth}
\end{figure}

AI adoption is growing rapidly, driving demand for data centers and potentially
stressing electrical infrastructure in coming
years~\cite{lbl:dc:2024,iea:ai:energy,iea:keyquestions:ai}. For ordinary chatbot
use, however, emissions remain a relatively small addition to the UZH IT
footprint. In our default scenario of 15 queries/user/day at 1 Wh/query,
ordinary chat contributes \ModelChatT{} \tcoe/year, or \ModelChatPercent{}\% of total
IT emissions, after including training overhead and amortized hardware
emissions.

Moreover, under aggressive ordinary-chat growth projections (50\% YoY usage
growth, 50\% YoY growth in training-overhead emissions, and a moderate 30\% YoY
improvement in inference efficiency), the central ordinary-chat scenario reaches
\ModelChatFinalT{} \tcoe/year in 2030, the fifth plotted year, or about 3--4\%
of the baseline broad IT footprint. Varying initial query energy from 0.3 to 3 Wh and initial volume from 10 to
30 queries/user/day gives \ModelChatFinalLowT{}--\ModelChatFinalHighT{}
\tcoe/year in that year;
growth rates remain fixed. See Figure~\ref{fig:llm:growth}. This result is consistent with other
analyses that put simple LLM use in the context of other computer
activity~\cite{epoch2025howmuchenergydoeschatgptuse,ritchie:chatgpt,ritchie:ai:electricity:2026}.

Agentic AI is different. The main result figures therefore split AI into ``AI
(chatbot)'' and ``AI (agents)''. The agent bar represents an illustrative
scenario in which 10\% of the UZH population uses coding or research agents
heavily on workdays at 1.5 facility kWh/user-day, yielding \ModelAgentDefaultT{} \tcoe/year
once facility overhead, training and amortized hardware are included. If 10\% used such
systems at 3 facility kWh/user-day, emissions would be \ModelAgentHighT{} \tcoe/year,
comparable to major existing operational IT categories.
Figure~\ref{fig:agentic:ai} shows the sensitivity of annual emissions to both
adoption and intensity. The conclusion is therefore not that all AI use is
negligible, but that emissions depend strongly on whether usage remains
dominated by simple chat or shifts toward high-intensity agentic work.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:sensitivity:analysis}

The results above are robust for ordinary chat use, but more conditional for
high-intensity agentic AI.

For example, if we (1) massively reduce the set of end-user devices in scope to
just those owned and managed by the university (about 10,000 laptops and
monitors, 2,400 mobile phones), (2) triple the ordinary LLM per-query energy use
to 3 Wh, and (3) assume that each of the 38,000 people at UZH uses a
GPT-4o-class LLM 30 times per day, then emissions from ordinary chat are about
\ModelSensOneChatPercent{}\% of total (Figure~\ref{fig:sens1}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig-sens1.png}
  \caption{Sensitivity scenario with university-managed laptop and phone counts, and high ordinary-chat use (30 queries/user/day at 3 Wh/query). Other assumptions, including the six-year laptop lifetime and illustrative agent use, are retained. Whiskers vary the same inputs as in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline}; this is separate from the sustainability-report comparison.}
  \label{fig:sens1}
\end{figure}

Similarly, if we cut our estimate for the embodied emissions of every type of
device to the lowest value found in the literature (as opposed to a more
representative middle-of-the-road value), but keep all ordinary-chat parameters
at their default values, embodied hardware emissions account for
\ModelLowDeviceEmbodiedPercent{}\% of the total and simple LLM emissions
continue to be a small fraction of the total, as seen in
Figure~\ref{fig:sens2}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig-sens2.png}
  \caption{Sensitivity scenario using the literature minimum for each local-device embodied footprint. Operational inputs and independent network, cloud, CSCS, and AI hardware proxies remain fixed. Whiskers vary the same inputs as in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline}; they do not describe a statistical distribution of device footprints.}
  \label{fig:sens2}
\end{figure}

Combining the ``low embodied emissions'' scenario with the ``pessimal ordinary
LLM'' scenario makes simple generative AI visible but still not dominant,
contributing \ModelCombinedChatPercent{}\% of total emissions, or
\ModelCombinedWithoutAgentsPercent{}\% if the illustrative
agentic-AI bar is excluded from the denominator.

Regional context matters as expected. Moving from the Swiss electricity
intensity of 53.75 \gcoekwh\ to 207 or 400 \gcoekwh\ increases local operational
emissions by factors of about 3.9 or 7.4. This change affects campus devices,
servers, and networking; external services retain their separate factors.
Embodied emissions remain important for end-user devices, while operation
becomes more prominent when the relevant serving regions have higher-carbon
electricity. Shorter device refresh cycles push in the opposite direction:
they increase the embodied-emissions share and make device-lifetime policy more
important.

Networking, productivity SaaS, and CSCS do not change the conclusion that device
lifetimes are central, but they are large enough to matter. The bottom-up
networking estimate implies roughly 50--100 \tcoe/year operational emissions
plus \ModelNetworkHardwareT{} \tcoe/year of embodied emissions, with
\ModelNetworkTotalT{} \tcoe/year
shown in the figures. The preliminary productivity-SaaS estimate spans 38--380
\tcoe/year, with 209 \tcoe/year shown in the figures. The CSCS scenario spans
\ModelCscsLowT{}--\ModelCscsHighT{} \tcoe/year over the declared PUE and
server-equivalent power bounds; the central result is \ModelCscsTotalT{}
\tcoe/year. These categories should therefore be included in future UZH
inventories even if they are not the dominant source in the baseline.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recommendations}

Our recommendations for university policy makers can be summarized in four main
points.

First, treat device lifetimes as a powerful lever for reducing the impact of IT.
European circular-economy guidance emphasizes repair, reuse, procurement
requirements, and delayed obsolescence as practical ways to reduce the impact of
electronics~\cite{eea:longer:electronics,eea:product:lifespans}. In the model,
starting with industry-standard lifetimes of 4 years for laptops and 3 years for
mobile phones, then extending those to 6 and 4 years, respectively, reduces
total campus IT emissions from \ModelLifetimeBeforeKt{}~\ktcoe\ to
\ModelLifetimeAfterKt{}~\ktcoe, a \ModelLifetimeSavingPercent{}\% reduction
(\ModelLifetimeSavingT{} \tcoe/year), holding other assumptions fixed.
Practical policies include minimum 5-year warranty and repairability
requirements in procurement, centrally supported repair services, reuse or
buy-back programs for students and staff, and default redeployment of
still-functional devices before new purchases.

Second, improve institutional data collection for categories that are currently
outside standard campus inventories. Universities should not only keep good
inventories of their own IT infrastructure and use, but also request
tenant-level reports from cloud SaaS providers such as Microsoft and Google.
This data is important for prioritizing interventions to improve overall IT
efficiency.

Third, manage AI by workload intensity rather than treating all AI use as
equivalent. Ordinary chatbot use has a small footprint in our central scenario, but agentic AI,
if broadly adopted, could become a significant contributor to overall IT
emissions. Universities procuring AI tools should ask providers for more
transparency into agentic AI energy consumption, including energy per task by
workload class, model-routing policies, context-caching behavior,  and whether
routine steps can default to smaller models. Campus policy should encourage
right-sizing models, avoiding unnecessary long-context or agent loops, and
measuring aggregate use of coding and research agents where privacy permits.

Fourth, consider cloud migration via a structured decision framework rather than
a generic assumption that cloud is greener. Migration is most compelling when it
replaces inefficient local cooling without increasing the number or intensity of
servers. Accurate accounting requires asking cloud providers for
location-specific electricity carbon intensity, annual PUE, hardware lifetime,
utilization, and tenant-level reporting. If cloud migration is more expensive
than on-premise operation, the opportunity cost should be compared with
higher-impact measures such as device-lifetime extension or building-energy
projects.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related:work}

Emissions modeling of information technology at organizational scales remains
underdeveloped. Universities seem to lack standardized frameworks for measuring
IT-related carbon footprints, and our work is in part an effort to help close
this gap.

Studies such as \textcite{larsen:2013} assess the carbon footprint of a
university but do not consider IT-related emissions specifically. For example,
Larsen et al. lump IT with ``equipment'', including machinery and science and
technology equipment.

Many carbon footprint studies treat IT as a minor subcomponent, despite IT
infrastructure representing 5-10\% of total institutional emissions. For
example, \textcite{herth:quantifying:2022} make a comprehensive carbon
assessment of TU Delft and find that ICT constitutes 8\% of emissions, but do
not break that down further, making it difficult to know how to improve.
\textcite{helmers:universities:2021} exclude IT impacts from their 20-university
comparison study, claiming that ``almost all universities worldwide will be
working with quite similar IT technology''. \textcite{kiehle:2023} include IT
equipment in a carbon accounting of the University of Oulu but limit themselves
to lifecycle analysis of equipment purchased in the last 3 years.

We are not alone in struggling with data availability problems, including
incomplete asset inventories, sparse energy consumption data, etc.
\textcite{jurgens:2023} writes that ``data availability is the main limiting
factor for a holistic assessment'' of higher education institutions (not limited
to IT).

Efforts to standardize IT emissions measurement and reporting are advancing,
\eg, through standards such as ITU-T L.1420, but they remain
fragmented~\cite{wb:itu:ict}. Our own research on embodied and operational
emissions revealed massive variability in carbon reporting among manufacturers
and across studies: system boundaries range from cradle-to-gate through
cradle-to-grave, functional units vary from per-device to per-computation, and
values for the same parameter might vary by a factor of 2 or more.

In this study, our analysis has been bottom up, building a model of the
university's IT emissions from individual estimates of devices, queries, etc.
Some other studies, including \textcite{larsen:2013} and
\textcite{garciaalaminos:2022}, have used top-down approaches based on
environmental input-output (EIO) models. For example, García-Alaminos et al. use
a multi-regional input-output framework (EEMRIO) to estimate the categories
(scopes 1, 2, and 3) of a university's carbon footprint based on analysis of
expenditures and other financial data. There are important tradeoffs between
bottom-up precision and top-down comprehensiveness~\cite{hilty:2009:lca}.
Bottom-up approaches provide higher accuracy for specific sources and
transparency through clear traceability. They also make it easier to identify
specific improvement areas. However, they require extensive primary data
collection, force up-front decisions about what components to include or not,
and may miss system-wide effects. Conversely, top-down approaches offer
efficiency and a broader system perspective, but sacrifice the precision and
granularity necessary for operational decision-making.

The field of AI is evolving rapidly and the main AI vendors are secretive about
the architecture and scale of their models. This has led to many efforts to
estimate the footprint of LLMs, from academic papers to blog
posts~\cite{devries2023growing,heguerte2023estimate,luccioni2022estimating,ritchie:chatgpt,epoch2025howmuchenergydoeschatgptuse,ritchie:ai:electricity:2026}.
More generally, the energy consumption of LLMs is being studied
extensively~\cite{budennyy2022eco2ai,castano2023exploring,epoch2024optimallyallocatingcomputebetweeninferenceandtraining,gowda2024watt,harding2024watts,luccioni2023counting,patterson2021carbon,rodriguez2024evaluating,tripp2024measuring,jegham2025hungry}.
\textcite{lacoste:mlco2} and \textcite{faiz2024llmcarbon} have developed more
systematic approaches to estimate the footprint of models based on factors like
electricity emissions intensity, hardware specifications, and network
architecture. Agentic systems are newer and less well covered by peer-reviewed
measurement; transparent case studies such as
\textcite{couch:claude:code:energy} are useful primarily for scenario
construction rather than as universal baselines.

\textcite{watershed:2026} proposes a framework for estimating organizational
emissions from purchased AI services. Its accounting boundary includes
inference, model training, data-center overhead, and embodied hardware
emissions. It distinguishes three calculation tiers based on available data:
expenditure-based estimates, activity-based estimates using token volumes, and
provider-reported emissions intensities. The framework highlights how
differences in accounting boundaries, production deployment, and allocation
assumptions can produce widely varying estimates for the same workload. Its
focus on organizational AI accounting complements our assessment of university
IT, in which we consider AI services alongside other IT infrastructure and
end-user devices.

In a provocative and maybe dystopian paper, \textcite{tomlinson:2024:carbon}
argue that AI has lower emissions than humans for various writing and
illustrating tasks. Other indirect effects may be beneficial, such as optimized
building operation, or harmful, such as rebound demand from cheaper content
generation. These systemic effects are important for a cleaner-production
strategy but fall outside the scope of this paper.

\textcite{sterman:2026} examine AI's net climate impact through a framework that
considers its direct footprint, effects on economic growth, and potential
contributions to emissions reductions. They emphasize that efficiency
improvements can stimulate additional demand, while benefits from AI-enabled
innovation may emerge only after substantial delays. Their analysis illustrates
why a small direct footprint does not by itself establish a favorable net
climate impact.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented a simple but powerful model to estimate the carbon footprint
of IT infrastructure and IT-related activities at the University of Zürich.
While the concrete parameter values we chose are specific to UZH, we have
attempted to create a framework that can be applied to other universities (and
maybe other large campus facilities) and can help provide a basis for evaluation
and planning.

Our model has broad system boundaries: it includes both university- and
student-owned devices, cloud and supercomputing facilities, productivity SaaS,
and generative-AI services. Whenever possible it accounts for both embodied and
operational emissions. The model is bottom-up and exposes almost 100
configurable parameters to support different environments and enable exploration
of different scenarios. A complete parameter inventory is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:parameters}. The model is available at
\url{https://maxp.net/it-emissions} and can be freely modified under an MIT
License.

Within the context of UZH, our analysis shows that, at least on the Swiss grid,
embodied emissions dominate the IT carbon footprint, while operational impacts
are comparatively modest but not negligible. In particular:

\begin{itemize}

  \item Under our estimates of 32,000 laptops and 35,000 phones across students
  and staff, embodied emissions of end-user devices
  represent \ModelEndUserEmbodiedPercent{}\% of the broad university IT footprint.
  Operational emissions account for \ModelEndUserOperationalPercent{}\% of total emissions of end-user devices
  (laptops, monitors, phones, etc.).

  \item Embodied emissions of end-user devices remain the largest category even
  when we consider only university-owned devices (equivalent to about
  $\frac{1}{4}$ of the population), although the share falls once SaaS and
  supercomputing are included in the broad boundary.

  \item Data center cooling and efficiency represent a potential savings
  opportunity. The model assigns \ModelLocalOverheadT{} \tcoe/year to local
  facility overhead; the full cloud-migration scenario saves
  \ModelCloudSavingT{} \tcoe/year overall. Any such move requires in-depth analysis with the
  cloud vendor to ensure that it is still a net savings after accounting for the
  different server infrastructure and other changes.

  \item Ordinary chatbot use contributes \ModelChatPercent{}\% of total IT emissions
  in our central scenario, including training and embodied hardware allocations.
  Agentic AI is more usage-sensitive: heavy adoption by 10\% of the university
  population could add hundreds of \tcoe/year, so future AI impact depends on
  workload mix rather than query counts alone.

\end{itemize}

The sensitivity scenarios show why these priorities depend on device footprints,
lifetimes, and workload intensity. The corresponding policy recommendations in
Section~\ref{sec:recommendations} emphasize lengthening device lifecycles,
improving data collection and procurement processes, and implementing
workload-aware AI governance.
